*Published in IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters, vol. 11, no. 9, pp. 10377–10384, September 2026. [DOI: 10.1109/LRA.2026.3710327](https://doi.org/10.1109/LRA.2026.3710327).*

Group synchronization sounds specialized, but the problem is familiar to many GTSAM users. Pose-graph SLAM estimates global robot poses from noisy relative poses. Rotation averaging recovers camera orientations from pairwise matches. Closely related problems appear in monocular SLAM with scale drift, $SL(d)$-based 3D reconstruction, invariant filtering on $SE_2(3)$, and moving reference frames modeled with the Galilean group.

In each case, we have unknown group elements $X_1,\ldots,X_N\in G$ and noisy measurements $\widetilde X_{ij}\approx X_i^{-1}X_j$. The aim is to recover a globally consistent set of states, up to a common gauge transformation.

**FAST-Sync is a batch initializer for this problem over matrix Lie groups.** It replaces the nonlinear problem with a structured linear approximation, solves that approximation efficiently, projects the answer back to the chosen group, and hands the result to a local optimizer such as Gauss-Newton or Levenberg-Marquardt.

That last step is essential. FAST-Sync is not a replacement for nonlinear optimization; it is a way to start nonlinear optimization in a much better place.

## Why initialization is the hard part

The maximum-likelihood synchronization problem is usually high-dimensional and non-convex. GTSAM's local optimizers can solve large instances efficiently, but—as with local optimization generally—the result depends on the initial estimate. A poor start can require many iterations to repair, or it can place the optimizer in the wrong basin of attraction.

A common baseline is to select a spanning tree and concatenate relative measurements outward from a root. This is very cheap, but every edge contributes error. Along a long tree path, those errors accumulate.

## From a manifold problem to a linear start

FAST-Sync generalizes the philosophy of chordal initialization beyond familiar rotation and rigid-pose groups. Its first move is to replace the original Log-map likelihood with a quadratic Frobenius-norm surrogate:

$$
J_F(X)=
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}
\kappa_{ij}
\left\|X_j-X_i\widetilde X_{ij}\right\|_F^2,
$$

where $\kappa_{ij}=\sigma_{ij}^{-2}$ is the inverse noise variance of measurement $(i,j)$ under the isotropic concentrated-Gaussian model we use in the paper. FAST-Sync then temporarily relaxes the constraints $X_i\in G$, solves for matrices in the surrounding ambient vector space, and projects them back to $G$.

The residual is linear in the unknown matrix entries. Following the notation in our paper and suppressing the scalar row weight, an edge $(i,j)$ defines the sparse block row

$$
(H_{ij})_\ell=
\begin{cases}
-\widetilde X_{ij}^{T}, & \ell=i,\\
I_d, & \ell=j,\\
0_{d\times d}, & \text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\qquad
\overline H=H\otimes I_d.
$$

The Kronecker product is the key computational observation. For nonunit weights, the corresponding edge rows are scaled by $\sqrt{\kappa_{ij}}$ in the weighted least-squares system. The Kronecker structure replaces the $d^2M\times d^2N$ vectorized system with $H\in\mathbb{R}^{Md\times Nd}$. After appending the gauge row, FAST-Sync factors the reduced, gauge-augmented matrix $A\in\mathbb{R}^{(M+1)d\times Nd}$ and reuses its triangular factor in the column-wise recovery.

Our first-order small-noise analysis motivates the Frobenius surrogate. For common groups including $SO(d)$, $SE(d)$, and $SU(2)$, the resulting quadratic approximation equals the matrix-space residual above. For more general matrix Lie groups, we use it as a practical heuristic for initialization—not as an exact replacement for the final manifold objective.

## Let the graph choose the gauge

For a connected synchronization graph, applying one common global transformation to every state leaves all relative measurements unchanged. A linear initializer therefore needs to fix one gauge state, usually to the identity. A disconnected graph would require one gauge per connected component.

In the noiseless problem, the choice of root is algebraically arbitrary. In the noisy ambient-space relaxation, however, the ordering affects both sparse fill and how far intermediate matrices drift from the group before projection.

FAST-Sync uses a **block-preserving Nested Dissection** ordering. The $d\times d$ entries belonging to one state always move together, while Nested Dissection recursively separates the measurement graph using small vertex separators. The final state in the reordered system becomes the identity gauge.

After sparse factorization, the remaining blocks are recovered in reverse order:

$$
\widehat X_N=I_d,\qquad
\widehat X_j=
-\left(
\sum_{k\in\mathrm{nz}_j}
\widehat X_kR_{jk}^{T}
\right)R_{jj}^{-T},
\qquad
X_j^0=\Pi_G(\widehat X_j).
$$

Here, the $R_{jk}$ are blocks of the sparse triangular factor and $\mathrm{nz}_j$ contains the later nonzero blocks needed to recover state $j$. In an implementation, the final multiplication is performed with a triangular solve rather than by forming an inverse.

The ambient estimates $\widehat X_j$ need not lie exactly on $G$, so each is rounded with a group-specific projection $\Pi_G$. For $SO(d)$, we use a polar-decomposition projection. Other groups need their own appropriate projection.

Nested Dissection helps twice: it reduces fill in the sparse factorization, and in our experiments it tends to choose a central root with a shallower elimination dependency graph. The second benefit is empirical and especially interesting—an ordering normally treated as a speed detail also influences initialization quality. A full theory of that effect remains open.

## FAST-Sync is now in GTSAM

In our paper, we mention GTSAM as one possible toolkit for the final Gauss-Newton or Levenberg-Marquardt refinement. The merged [GTSAM PR #2634](https://github.com/borglab/gtsam/pull/2634) adds FAST-Sync directly to the `develop` branch. In C++, [`fastSync<T>(graph)`](https://github.com/borglab/gtsam/blob/develop/gtsam/slam/FastSync.h) accepts a `NonlinearFactorGraph` and returns a `Values` object that can be passed directly to a nonlinear optimizer. The public `FastSync<T>` class also exposes the ambient solve and projection/alignment stages separately.

The implementation supports `Rot2`, `Rot3`, `Pose2`, `Pose3`, `Similarity2`, `Similarity3`, and `SL4`. The $SU(2)$ and $Gal(3)$ cases in our experiments below are not among the current wrapped types. Generated Python and MATLAB entry points use type-suffixed names such as `fastSyncRot3` and `fastSyncPose3`. The repository includes both a [technical notebook](https://github.com/borglab/gtsam/blob/develop/gtsam/slam/doc/FastSync.ipynb), which explains the reduced solve and projection interface, and a [runnable Python tutorial](https://github.com/borglab/gtsam/blob/develop/python/gtsam/examples/FastSyncExample.ipynb), which covers all seven supported groups and subsequent Levenberg-Marquardt refinement. A [MATLAB example](https://github.com/borglab/gtsam/blob/develop/matlab/gtsam_examples/FastSyncExample.m) exercises the same seven type-specific functions.

The API reads matching `BetweenFactor<T>` measurements from the graph and accepts at most one matching `PriorFactor<T>`. Each between factor must use a `noiseModel::Isotropic` model of the correct tangent dimension with a finite, positive sigma, and the measurement graph must be non-empty and connected. By default, METIS Nested Dissection selects the identity gauge; after the complete ambient solve, the matrices are projected to `T` and, when a matching prior is present, aligned to it with one common left transformation. Additional fixed-size matrix Lie groups can opt in by specializing `FastSyncProjection<T>`.

If the final factor graph uses anisotropic or robust noise models, a separate isotropic synchronization graph can be used for FAST-Sync, and its returned `Values` can then initialize the final graph. FAST-Sync remains a batch initializer; using it to bootstrap a connected graph or submap before iSAM2 is still a separate deployment choice. The implementation currently lives on the `develop` branch after the GTSAM 4.3a2 prerelease; it is not included in GTSAM 4.2.2 or 4.3a2.

### Cholesky and Shonan averaging

A [follow-up PR #2639](https://github.com/borglab/gtsam/pull/2639) proposes replacing the initial implementation's sequential QR elimination with a Cholesky-preferred path, allowing either a selected ordering type or a complete custom ordering, and making FAST-Sync with METIS the default initializer for Shonan averaging. When Shonan's direct optimizer needs an ordering, the proposed implementation reuses the same ordering through the Riemannian staircase.

The follow-up's current YFCC2 benchmark measures one Shonan PCG run on each of 61 datasets, using the largest connected component when a graph is disconnected. Aggregate initialization time drops from 17.506 s with chordal initialization and COLAMD to 4.015 s with FAST-Sync and METIS, a 4.36× speedup. Total initialization-plus-solve time drops from 41.574 s to 30.495 s, a 1.36× speedup, and all 122 before/after runs certify at rank 3. This comparison changes both the initializer and the ordering; it does not isolate the effect of Cholesky. These are subsequent GTSAM implementation measurements, separate from the experiments in our paper below.

## What our experiments show

We evaluate FAST-Sync on six groups: $SO(3)$, $SE(3)$, $SU(2)$, $SL(4)$, $Gal(3)$, and $Sim(3)$. In our synthetic small-world experiments, maximum-spanning-tree initialization is cheaper at the first step, but accumulated error often leaves much more work for the nonlinear optimizer. FAST-Sync pays more up front and frequently wins on total time because refinement starts closer to the desired basin. For $SO(3)$, $SE(3)$, and $SU(2)$, we use SE-Sync to obtain the certifiably global reference solution. For $SL(4)$, $Gal(3)$, and $Sim(3)$, we estimate the global optimum using local optimization initialized from ground truth.

In our representative-dataset study, we see the same overall pattern on the Intel $SE(2)$ pose graph, the Rim $SE(3)$ dataset, Pantheon rotation averaging, and a $Gal(3)$ Drone graph derived from a real drone sequence but populated with synthetic relative measurements. The practical value is not that FAST-Sync's linear solve is always the cheapest initialization. It is that **initialization plus refinement** can be faster and more reliable than beginning with a poorer estimate.

## Boundaries and open questions

“Any matrix Lie group” describes the reusable linear-algebraic template. It does not mean that every group is automatically handled without additional work. A new group still needs a usable matrix representation, a projection $\Pi_G$, and appropriate manifold operations and factors for refinement.

FAST-Sync itself does not provide a global-optimality certificate. In our experiments, it often places a local optimizer in the reference solution's basin, but this remains an empirical result rather than a guarantee. We study an isotropic Gaussian model and additional injected noise; we do not evaluate robustness to gross outliers. Robust losses, outlier rejection, or richer covariance models in a subsequent GTSAM refinement are reasonable engineering extensions, but they are outside the scope of our experiments.

## Takeaway

FAST-Sync turns group synchronization into a strong starting estimate by combining five ideas: an ambient Frobenius surrogate, Kronecker reduction, block-preserving Nested Dissection, sparse back-substitution, and projection to the group. The result is not the end of the optimization pipeline. It gives a local optimizer a stronger starting point and, in many of our experiments, reduces total initialization-plus-refinement runtime.

The new GTSAM implementation makes that initializer available directly from a factor graph. The Shonan follow-up integrates FAST-Sync's estimate and reuses its ordering within a certifiable rotation-averaging pipeline.
